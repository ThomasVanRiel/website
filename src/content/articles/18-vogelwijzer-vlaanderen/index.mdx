Bird observations do not only describe birds. They also describe birdwatchers. A popular reserve can accumulate many reports while an equally suitable, rarely visited place remains almost empty in the data. Raw observations therefore cannot be read as a distribution map without accounting for where and when people looked.

I built [Vogelwijzer Vlaanderen](https://vogelwijzer.thomasvanriel.com/) to separate those two patterns as far as the available data allows. It combines a reproducible data pipeline, a spatial and seasonal model, preregistered validation, and a static web application. The result shows where and when a species is reported more or less often than its own average, together with the amount of evidence behind every estimate.

## The source data

The model starts with the public Waarnemingen.be archive. After restricting the data to 2008–2018 and applying the documented taxonomic filters, the training set contains **11,501,933 observations** across **645 5×5 km grid cells** and 52 weeks of the year.

The archive contains presence records, not complete checklists. It does not publish an observer identifier, visit duration, distance travelled, or an event identifier that could group observations into visits. That rules out methods which need known visits or explicit search effort.

The observer bias is visible in the archive itself:

- the busiest 10% of cells contain **49.8%** of all records;
- reporting per weekend day is **1.66×** reporting per weekday;
- reporting activity varies **3.14×** between the quietest and busiest weeks.

These measurements are regenerated by the pipeline rather than entered into the application by hand.

## Using birds to measure birdwatching

Reports of all birds in a cell and week provide a proxy for how much birdwatching happened there. The model treats the number of reports for one species as a share of that total:

```text
y[i,j,k] ~ Binomial(n[j,k], p[i,j,k])
logit(p[i,j,k]) = intercept + cyclic season[k] + spatial field[j]
```

Here, `y` is the number of reports for species *i*, while `n` is the number of all bird reports in cell *j* and week *k*. A zero among three bird reports is weak evidence. A zero among nine hundred reports carries much more weight.

The seasonal term is a cyclic second-order random walk over 52 weeks. The spatial term is a BYM2 areal field over neighbouring cells. Both are fitted together with INLA. This approach is a **target-group background correction**: other bird reports serve as the background against which a species' reporting pattern is estimated.

## What the maps mean

The output is a **relative reporting rate**, not a probability of presence and not an estimate of abundance. A value of `2.4×` means that, after accounting for total bird-reporting activity, the species' share of reports is 2.4 times its own average across Flanders and the year.

This makes comparisons useful **within one species**: one place against another, or one week against another. It does not make unrestricted comparisons between species valid. Common species may be under-reported while conspicuous or unusual species may be over-reported, and the source data cannot fully correct that species-specific preference.

Every published estimate retains two support counts: reports of the selected species and reports of all birds. The interface hatches cells with too little support instead of colouring them. A place where nobody looked must not appear to be a place without birds.

## Testing before publishing

The evaluation plan and its thresholds were [preregistered](https://vogelwijzer.thomasvanriel.com/methode/preregistratie) before the full model was fitted. The spatial model was compared with a season-only null model in two ways.

Five-fold spatial block cross-validation **failed**. The spatial random effect could not extrapolate into contiguous regions for which it had no observations; there it reverted towards its prior and could perform worse than the season-only model.

External validation used standardised ABV and Watervogels surveys that were never used for training. In cells supported by the training data, **114 of the 243 fitted species** passed the frozen publication gates. The failed cross-validation remains part of the result: the model can recover useful patterns where it has evidence, but it cannot manufacture reliable maps for unobserved regions.

The application exposes that distinction instead of giving every species the same output. Its generated index currently contains:

- **114 validated** species with a map and seasonal curve;
- **47 modelled** species with enough support but no suitable external validation data;
- **82 seasonal-only** species that do not receive a map;
- **233 species with insufficient data**, which remain listed without an estimate.

## From model to browser

The project is split at a versioned data contract:

1. DuckDB SQL turns the source archive into canonical observations, reporting-activity surfaces, and model input.
2. R and INLA fit the seasonal and spatial models and run validation.
3. The pipeline applies the frozen publication gates and exports the approved outputs as partitioned Parquet files.
4. A static SvelteKit application reads those files directly in the browser with Hyparquet.

The data cube is exported in two orientations: one partition per species and one per grid cell. Selecting a species or a cell downloads only the relevant partition, rather than the complete set of modelled surfaces. The application needs no database or application server.

The [technical methods](https://vogelwijzer.thomasvanriel.com/methode/technisch) describe the model, tests, results, and limitations in full. The complete pipeline, application, preregistration, and data contract are available in the [source repository](https://github.com/ThomasVanRiel/vogelwijzer).
