FRAMING FOR THE WHOLE PIECE. The site's lane is technical + empirical + visual.
There are a thousand "how the seasons work" explainers with a tilted-Earth
cartoon; this is not one. The spine here is: **the sun's position is exactly two
angles, and every seasonal intuition you have is a projection of a shape those
two angles trace.** Show the shape, then explain it.

Everything is Leuven-specific and stated as such: **50.88° N, 4.70° E**. Not a
generic "mid-latitude" article — concrete numbers for one place people can walk
around in.

CONVENTION, state it once and hold it everywhere (figures included):
- **Elevation** = angle above the horizon. Horizon = 0°, zenith = +90°,
  below the horizon is negative.
- **Azimuth** = angle along the horizon, **south = 0°**. The sign follows the
  sun through the day: **negative all morning, zero at solar noon, positive all
  afternoon.** −90° = due east, +90° = due west, north = ±180°. Say it in those
  words — "the number counts up as the day runs" is the whole convention, and
  it means the sign of the azimuth alone tells you morning from evening.
- Flag explicitly that this is the *astronomical/solar* convention and that
  navigation and most software use north = 0° clockwise, so anyone comparing
  against a library gets a 180° surprise. Give the one-line conversion.

Write 3–4 sentences of intro before the questions: the "step outside and point
at the sun" hook, the claim that its whole position is two numbers, and that a
year of those numbers has a shape.

## What actually moves

<Scaffold>
The minimum astronomy needed to make the plots legible, and no more. Keep it to
three moving parts:

1. **Rotation** — 15°/hour, gives the daily arc.
2. **Axial tilt, 23.44°** — the declination of the sun swings ±23.44° over the
   year; this is the whole of "seasons".
3. **Orbital eccentricity + the tilt again** — gives the equation of time,
   which is why solar noon wanders.

The one equation worth writing out in KaTeX (remark-math is already wired up):
the solar elevation formula

  sin(h) = sin(φ)sin(δ) + cos(φ)cos(δ)cos(H)

with φ = latitude, δ = declination, H = hour angle. Then immediately do the
sanity substitutions for Leuven: H = 0 (solar noon) collapses it to
h = 90° − φ + δ, which hands you 62.6° in June and 15.7° in December. Show that
arithmetic — it is the moment the reader realises they can now compute this
themselves.

Azimuth formula: mention it exists, give it, but do not derive it. The quadrant
disambiguation (atan2) is a footnote-level detail.

DECISION TO MAKE: how much rigour? Options are (a) the simple circular-orbit
approximation, which is off by a fraction of a degree and fits in one code
block, or (b) NOAA / Meeus-grade formulas, accurate to arcseconds. Leaning (a)
for the prose with a note that the *figures* use (b). Verify before asserting
any accuracy number.
</Scaffold>

across a wide stretch of horizon instead of sitting on E and W.

Alternative worth prototyping before committing: the **Cartesian** sun path
(x = azimuth running −180° → +180°, y = elevation) — the signed convention pays
off here, since time simply runs left to right across the plot. Less beautiful, far easier to read angles off,
and it plays better on a phone. Possibly do both — polar as the hero, Cartesian
as the reference.

## The sun keeps its own time

<Scaffold>
The equation of time section. Two causes, kept separate:
- the elliptical orbit (Earth moves faster at perihelion, early January)
- the axial tilt (projection of the ecliptic onto the equator)

They combine into a ±~16 minute wobble. Add Leuven's longitude correction:
4.70° E against the 15° E meridian of CET means the clock is offset by roughly
41 minutes from local solar time before DST is even considered — worth checking
and stating exactly.

Then the payoff: solar noon in Leuven never lands at 12:00. Computed over a
full year it runs from **12:25 to 13:48** on the clock, and the equation of
time alone swings from **−14.2 to +16.5 minutes**. The 41-minute longitude
correction — 4 minutes per degree, times the 10.3° between Leuven and the 15° E
meridian that defines CET — is the constant part; the equation of time and DST
are what make it move.

FIGURE 5 — **the analemma.** Plot the sun's position at a fixed clock time
across the year and the figure-eight appears. This is the reward for the whole
section and it is a genuinely startling shape the first time you see it. Fixed
clock time, stated on the figure, and note that changing that time slides the
whole eight along the sky.
</Scaffold>

Leuven"; it is a single instrument the prose drives, and the reader can scrub
latitude from the equator to the pole and watch every figure deform together.
The prose should exploit that explicitly — "drag the latitude up to 66° and

TWO THINGS THE CONTROL EXPOSES, both worth prose:
- **The convention is fixed: south = 0°, everywhere.** Negative all morning, 0°
  at solar noon, +90° due west. It is stated once and never moves — no
  hemisphere-relative reference, which was tried and removed as more confusing
  than the thing it fixed. The honest consequence: south of the equator the noon
  sun passes to the NORTH, so Santiago's noon azimuth reads ±180° rather than 0°
  and its arcs sit at the edges of the scale rather than the middle. The plots
  break their paths at the ±180° seam instead of drawing a line across, so
  nothing renders wrong. This is a northern-hemisphere article and the
  convention is chosen for Leuven; one sentence acknowledging that is enough,
  and there is no need to dwell on it.

- **Clock times are real, DST included — for known locations.** Each preset
  carries an IANA zone and `zoneOffsetHours` asks the browser's own time-zone
  database (`Intl`, cached per zone per day), so summer time, political offsets
  and historical oddities are all exactly right with no dependency and no
  hand-written rules. A hand-typed longitude has no zone to look up, so it falls
  back to mean solar time for its meridian with **no DST at all** — the control
  says which of the two is in play. Latitude scrubbing keeps the zone (a zone is
  a longitude concept), so sweeping the slider never puts an hour's cliff in the
  middle of a sweep.

- **THE DST STEP IS NOW A REAL COMPARISON, and it is the best thing the location
  control buys the article.** Verified in the data: Leuven's sunrise jumps
  06:26 → 07:24 on 29 March; Santiago's jumps 06:55 → 07:54 on 6 September, the
  opposite end of the year; Reykjavík never steps at all. Three locations, three
  different answers, and the astronomy underneath is identical in all of them.
  That is the "one curve is astronomy, the kink in it is legislation" line,
  earned with evidence rather than asserted — write it here.

PROTOTYPE — this is the working interactive, not a placeholder. It merges what

- Math is `@lib/solar.ts`, NOAA algorithm, validated against published Leuven

- One accent colour only; every guide is recessive ink. Validated against the

## The whole year at once

<Scaffold>
PROTOTYPE — the spiral. The daily loop is one turn and the year is the radius,
so 365 turns wind outward from 1 January at the centre to 31 December at the
rim, with elevation as colour. It is the same data as the day plot above,
wrapped, and it makes the year's shape legible in a single glance in a way the
line charts cannot: the daylight region is a fat comma, the two curves bounding
it are sunrise and sunset, and the darkest core is high summer at noon.

WHY IT WORKS HERE: it is the one figure that shows *daily* and *annual*
periodicity at once, because the two are on different axes rather than fighting
for the same one. Everything in the rest of the article is a slice through it —
the day plot is one turn, the sun path is a re-projection, the analemma is what
you get reading down a single radius.

THINGS THE FIGURE HANDS THE PROSE FOR FREE (each worth a sentence or two):
- The daylight comma is **not symmetric about the vertical**, because solar noon
  is not at 12:00 — it visibly leans. This is the equation-of-time section's
  argument, made before the section arrives.
- The **DST step**. Sunrise and sunset jump by a full hour on the two changeover
  days, and in the spiral they show up as clean discontinuities in otherwise
  smooth curves. That is a genuinely nice thing to point at: one of the two
  curves is astronomy, the kink in it is legislation.
- The **earliest-sunset-is-not-the-solstice** effect is visible as the sunset
  curve turning before the sunrise curve does.

THE SECOND MODE ("Sky coil") IS THE WEAKER ONE — decide whether it survives:
it draws the same year in the sky itself, so each day closes into a loop
(night included, outside the horizon circle) and the year is those loops
migrating between the solstices. At true daily spacing consecutive loops sit a
fraction of a pixel apart and flood into a solid band, so it is currently drawn
every fifth day — 73 loops — which reads as a wound ball of string. Honest
assessment: it is pretty, it overlaps heavily with the sun-path plot above, and
it is harder to read than either. Options are (a) cut it, (b) keep it purely as
an ornament with a one-line caption and no analytical claims, or (c) rebuild it
as a proper 3D helix seen from outside the celestial sphere, which is the shape
people actually picture when they hear "the sun spirals". Leaning (b), with (c)
as the ambitious version.

THE ANIMATION: the chart is fully drawn from the start — colours, sunrise and
sunset curves, month ticks, all of it. The only thing that moves is the sun: a
dot travelling the spiral with a short trail behind it, the ring of the day it
is currently in held faintly under it. Day and time sliders both write to the
same cursor, so scrubbing either one moves the sun; speed runs from ¼ day/s
(the daily turn is clearly legible) to 20 days/s (the turn blurs and you read
the seasonal drift instead).

What the animation is FOR, and what the prose should say while it runs: at a
slow speed you watch a single day sweep and see how much of the turn is
daylight; speed it up and the turn blurs into a band that visibly breathes wider
and narrower. Watching the sun accumulate the year is a different claim from
seeing the year finished — it makes the *rate* of change legible, which is the
thing people get wrong (the swing is fastest at the equinoxes, not the
solstices). If that reads clearly on screen, say it in the prose right here.

DONE — the colour legend. A gradient bar with degree ticks plus a swatch for the
below-horizon grey now sits under the figure. The ramp is defined in fractions
of the local ceiling (the highest the sun ever gets at that latitude, 62.6° in
Leuven) rather than fixed angles, so it re-scales with the location field and
the top of the scale always means "as high as it ever gets here".

STILL TO DO ON THE SPIRAL ITSELF:
- Label the solstices and the equinoxes on the rim, so the eye has anchors.
- Decide whether midnight-at-top is right. Noon-at-top would put the daylight
  comma symmetric-ish about the vertical and might read better; midnight-at-top
  keeps the day unbroken across the bottom. Try both.
- Check it on a phone: it is a square figure with fine radial structure, and
  365 turns compressed into 300 px of radius is under a pixel per day at
  full width.

## Why anyone should care

<Scaffold>
Land the practical consequences — this is where the article earns its keep and
stops being an astronomy explainer. Candidates, pick 2–3 and go deep rather
than listing all of them:

- **Solar panels.** The optimal fixed tilt for Leuven ≈ latitude-ish; explain
  *why* via the cosine of the incidence angle, and why the winter sun costs you
  twice (low elevation AND short day AND more atmosphere to cross).
- **Buildings and shade.** Overhang depth that shades a south window in July and
  lets sun in in December is a solved problem that falls straight out of the
  62.6°/15.7° pair. This is the best example — it is a real design rule
  derived from two numbers in this article.
- **Photography.** Golden hour is an elevation band (roughly 0–6°), not a clock
  time — which is why it lasts twenty minutes in the tropics and most of an
  evening here in June. Ties to the site's photography section.

VERIFY every number in this section before publishing; these are the claims a
reader is most likely to act on.
