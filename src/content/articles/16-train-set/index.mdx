Wooden train tracks like BRIO, IKEA Lillabo, or one of their many compatible cousins are part of the toys in most households.
Anyone who has snapped together a wooden train track has some point stepped back to wonder whether the layout is any _good_.
There is a real instinct behind that question, as some tracks feel satisfying to run a train on, while others feel broken and need a lot of interaction.
While moving the train manually is fine, automatic trains that require a lot of interaction can get tiring, taking away attention from other parts of the playset, distributed in every other corner of the living room.
This article turns that instinct into something we can measure.

It's worth thinking of some questions we would like to answer to figure out if the train tracks are good, or actually measure the appeal of a track. These **research questions** are answered preliminary with a **hypothesis** to guide the answer.

## Graph theory, the math behind train tracks

**Graph theory** is a field of mathematics which studies connections between points or **nodes**.
The mathematician Leonhard Euler has used it in the 18th century to solve mathemathical puzzles.
[He wondered if he could cross the river in Koningsberg using the seven bridges, using every bridge exactly _once_.](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W18FDEA1jRQ)

### The track as a graph

When we look at our train track, we can see that it is a similar puzzle.
The points where the train has a **choice**, such as switches, are the nodes.
We can then simplify the track by combining all pieces in between two switches and calling them **edges**.
We have now simplified the many pieces of track into several nodes, connected by edges, which we call a **graph**.

You have probably seen this before, as the simplification is quite common in rail transport.
If we look at a **metro map** for instance, the stations are nodes where you have a choice (changing trains or hopping on or off), connected by a simple line.

We have a problem though, our switches are _not_ simple nodes. The train cannot go in every direction without reversing, which we have _forbidden_ in research question 1. Switches have a limited choice as the two branches are not connected to eachother, only to the trunk. **(Add figure of tree equivalent?)**
This seemingly simple constraint complicates the graph immensely, as we no longer have a simple graph. Transit is **symmetric or undirected** as trains can drive in two directions by design. The choice in the nodes on the other hand is **asymmetric**, the train only has a choice when entering from the trunk.

CRITICAL MODELING BEAT — a switch is NOT an ordinary graph node. An ordinary node means every
incident edge connects through to every other. A switch breaks that: it has a trunk (point end)
and two branches, and the allowed through-connections are trunk->branch1 and trunk->branch2 but
NEVER branch1->branch2. That is a "turn restriction" sitting on the node — the plain
junctions-as-nodes graph cannot express it. So the directedness here does not live on the edges;
it lives in WHICH PAIRS of edges connect at the node.

The fix (state it in one friendly sentence, no jargon dump): the real graph the train walks on has
a node for every segment-PLUS-direction (an oriented segment), and an edge for every legal move
between them. This is the directed line graph; same trick used for road networks with turn
restrictions. Two things happen at a switch and they are different:

- Transit is symmetric: enter the trunk -> leave on a branch; enter a branch -> forced onto the trunk.
- Choice is asymmetric: entering the trunk (facing) you CHOOSE a branch — this is where the
  randomness enters; entering a branch (trailing) you have NO choice. That choice-asymmetry is
  what lets a segment end up traversed in only one direction.

This one beat pays off three times later: (a) it justifies why direction matters at all, (b) it
sets up strong vs weak connectivity in the next subsection, and (c) it explains why the Markov
state is a DIRECTED segment. Keep it to ~2-3 sentences in the prose; the diagram should show a
switch with the branch->branch connection crossed out.

FORMAL TERMINOLOGY (do NOT put these terms in the body — too heavy; keep here as the precise
vocabulary for the deeper follow-up article). The track is really TWO graphs, and only one is a
digraph:

- Physical track = an undirected MULTIGRAPH WITH FORBIDDEN TRANSITIONS (a "transition system").
  Undirected because segments are bidirectional — direction is NOT a property of the edges.
  Multigraph because parallel segments and self-loops are possible. Vertices = junctions/endpoints;
  edges = segments. Endpoints are degree-1 (pendant/leaf) vertices = the "must reverse" points;
  pass-through joints degree-2; switches degree-3. The switch constraint is exactly a "forbidden
  transition": only certain pairs of incident edges connect through the vertex. This is the rigorous
  name for "directed but not in the conventional sense" — the constraint lives in the TRANSITIONS at
  the vertex, not on the edges. So the track is NOT a digraph.
- What the train walks / the math object = a genuine DIGRAPH. Build it by: (1) replace each segment
  with two antiparallel arcs (= the SYMMETRIC DIGRAPH of the track); (2) take its LINE DIGRAPH
  (vertices = oriented segments (segment,direction); arcs = legal moves) and prune the moves the
  transition system forbids. This "oriented-segment line digraph" is where digraph / strong
  connectivity / SCCs / the Markov chain properly live.
- Dynamics on it = a RANDOM WALK / MARKOV CHAIN on that digraph.

Audience-safe phrasing for the body (constructs the line digraph WITHOUT naming it): call the track
"an undirected graph", say "a switch is a special node where the branches don't connect to each
other", and "to follow the train we track each segment together with a direction". That's it — the
formal names (multigraph / forbidden transitions / symmetric digraph / line digraph) stay parked
here and in Future work.

Then: the parity constraint. Every piece has a male and a female end; the track must alternate
M -> F -> M -> F at every joint. Two male ends or two female ends can't connect. Adapter pieces
fix this. In the simulator, magic connectors replace real-life freeform fitting: they define a
specific path between two endpoints so the program can reason about the layout.

A track is a collection of straight pieces, curves, and switches connecting them. Switches are the interesting part: they're the points where the train has a _choice_. Once we draw each segment as an edge and each junction as a node, the question becomes a question about a graph.

There's a subtlety the graph alone hides: every real piece — in BRIO, Lillabo, and all compatible systems — has a "male" end and a "female" end, so a valid track has to alternate M → F → M → F at every joint. Two male ends or two female ends bumping into each other simply won't connect. That's a _parity_ constraint sitting on top of the graph — and it's exactly what adapter pieces are for.

### Connectivity

Remember the directedness we talked about in the previous section? There is an important connection we can make to the research questions. The first question we pose contains the _directedness_: _"Can the train reach everywhere **without changing direction**?"_.

We can translate this into the graph as well. When we place the train on the track, we do not care about the initial direction. The train can go either forward or backward, but still should be able to reach every other part of track. In graph theory, we call this property **strong connectivity**.

<Scaffold>
Introduce weak vs. strong connectivity with a concrete example before naming the terms. IMPORTANT:
apply them to the oriented-segment graph from the previous subsection, NOT to the junction graph —
that distinction is the whole point.

- Weak connectivity: the physical layout is one connected piece — ignore direction and you can get
  from anywhere to anywhere. Almost always true; the easy condition.
- Strong connectivity: on the directed oriented-segment graph, you can reach every (segment,
  direction) state from every other by legal moves — forward only, no lifting the train off the
  track. Genuinely stronger because the switch turn-restrictions can trap a direction.

The one-diagonal example is the clean SEPARATOR — lead with it: the track stays weakly connected
(still one physical piece) but is NOT strongly connected. The train crosses the diagonal once, the
choice it made fixes its orientation, and that state becomes unreachable forever. Two diagonals
restore strong connectivity. (This is also the dead-end-with-no-visible-stub finding used in the
Findings section.)

This is the formal answer to research question 1. Name the terms after the intuitive explanation,
not before.

FORWARD LINK to seed in one clause: strong connectivity here is the same property as irreducibility
of the Markov chain in the next section — "reaches every directed segment" = strongly connected =
irreducible. Don't belabor it, just plant it so the Markov section can pay it off.

A single-direction train cannot back up out of a dead end, so reaching every segment imposes a real constraint on the layout. The relevant graph property here is strong connectivity, and — if we want every _segment_ covered rather than every junction — we drift into Eulerian-circuit territory.

## Simplifying the mathematics by simulation

Graph theory tells us about the structure of the track, but not how much traffic each segment gets. For that, we **simulate**. We will build a virtual model of the track that mirrors the train set, and use the computer to run the train at very high speeds, much faster than we can run it ourselves. We are making a **digital twin** of the track, an identical copy of the physical track, fully in the computer. The digital twin allows us to check the track really quickly, even before building it, and is what engineers use to test real infrastructure projects.

### Markov chains

We have already described the track as a graph, but not how it should be driven by the train. After every segment, the train can have a choice. Entering the trunk of the switch, the train chooses between the left or the right branch. It comes from a track segment, and goes to another. The next segment only depends on where the train is **now**, not on the whole path the train has taken. We call this a **Markov Chain**.

The _strongly connected_ property of a graph transfers to Markov Chains, where it is called **irreducible**. If a segment can never be reached it can be removed, _reducing_ the track.

By describing the chance of a train taking a certain switch side, we can describe the track as a Markov Chain in a matrix as the state transitions are known. The train's path becoms a random walk on a graph of segments. We can then calculate a score for every segment in a _closed form_ solution to know how much it is visited by the train. Busy segments score high; isolated tracks score low.

### Monte Carlo, the digital twin

To form a better understanding of the behaviour of the train on the track, we will use a more visual method.
By running a virtual train, we can see the path the train takes on the track. When the train arrives at a switch, it has a chance to take the left track, or to take the right track. We will assume an unbiased choice, the train takes the left track half of the time, the right track the other half.

It is also possible to assign other chances to the switch branches, maybe the left track is taken 80% of the time. We can even extend the choice to even more choices if we have a switch with more than two exits. Or if we need to roll two dice to determine the chance, like in board games such as Catan, or Monopoly.

The result of the simulation is not as exact as the closed form solution but can get close _enough_ after a while. It takes time to accumulate sufficient track traversals. The extreme example is right at the start of the simulation: the train has only driven the first pieces, so all other parts of the track was never driven, we might wrongly conclude that they are never visited. Further on in the simulation, some track segments are _difficult_ to reach and are not _yet_ driven. It is important to run the simulation long enough, while at the same time considering the amount of time available to run the simulation.

The score of each segment is calculated the amount of steps in which the segment was visited, divided by the total number of steps the train took.

We use Monte Carlo in this problem to gain a visual intuition in Markov Chains and the Monte Carlo method, while an exact approach is available to score the tracks. Monte Carlo can also be applied in problems where an exact solution is not available. For weather forecasts for instance, meteorologists can run hundreds of slightly different simulation to generate forecasts. This is actually how we can know minimum and maximum temperatures, with an estimated temperature. This is why the estimates get less accurate the more in the future the forecast lies, more uncertainties have accumulated to skew the result.

In this article, the simulation is shown at a slow speed and might not give an accurate view of the Monte Carlo process. It is commonly ran multiple times at once instead of only one train at a time to save a lot of time.

The natural way to surface these per-segment probabilities on the track itself is to draw a shape that grows alongside each segment in proportion to its traffic. Splitting that shape into a _left_ half and a _right_ half — one bulge per direction of travel — turns out to reveal more than the totals alone.

A _symmetric_ bulge means the segment is a two-way street: the train passes through it about as often in each direction. A _lopsided_ bulge means the segment is effectively one-directional — the layout funnels traffic through it in only one orientation. A plain oval shows up as 100% one-sided everywhere; a balloon track shows up as a symmetric trunk feeding a one-sided loop. So the visualisation isn't just an aesthetic display of the score — the symmetry pattern surfaces the directional structure of the layout for free.

### Statistics

<Scaffold>
  The key statistic for a layout is **utilization**: what fraction of pieces are
  actually visited by the train in the long run? A piece that is never visited is
  dead weight — it takes up space on the floor but the train ignores it. A layout
  with 20 pieces where only 14 are visited has 70% utilization.

Each piece also has a directional sub-stat: is it used in both directions, or
only one? A piece used in both directions shows a symmetric bulge; a piece used
in one direction only shows a lopsided one. A piece with no bulge at all is not
utilized.

A plain oval is 100% utilized — every piece is visited — but entirely
one-directional: the train always goes the same way. A layout with switches can
be 100% utilized and bidirectional, meaning every piece is visited and in both
directions. That is the ideal.

**Stress that these statistics are stochastic and need time to settle.** The
simulation is a random walk, so the utilization and bidirectional percentages
are running estimates, not fixed readouts — they drift as the train runs and
are never truly final. Early on they read artificially high: the figure counts
every piece the train has touched so far, so a fresh run can sit at 100%
utilized before the long-run pattern emerges and idle segments drop out. Tell
the reader plainly to let each simulation run for a while before trusting the
numbers — the first few seconds are burn-in, not signal. They tend toward the
true value but approach it asymptotically.

</Scaffold>

### Quick tips for a good track

<Scaffold>
4-5 short, actionable rules for builders. Suggested starting point (from the old Key takeaways):

- More switches -> more even coverage. Every switch is a decision point that mixes the train.
- Spread switches around. Two adjacent switches give the train only one meaningful choice.
- Watch out for switches whose branches rejoin quickly — you're paying for a switch and getting
  a straight piece.
- Avoid dead ends if you want one-direction running.
- A loop with a spur beats a long straight line.

**Computing it exactly, deterministically.** DELIBERATELY CUT FROM THIS ARTICLE —

**Irreducibility.** The diagonal examples show that a layout can look connected
while hiding a structural trap — a segment reachable in one direction only,
which the train crosses once and never returns to. The formal property is
irreducibility: a chain is irreducible if every state is reachable from every
other state. An irreducible layout wastes no track. The interesting question is
then: what is the minimal set of pieces you would need to add (or reroute) to
make a given layout irreducible? That is a graph theory problem with a concrete,
hands-on answer.

**Richer traffic statistics.** Utilization tells you which pieces are used, but
not how unevenly the traffic is distributed among the ones that are. Two layouts
can both be 100% utilized while one has one wildly overloaded junction and
another has perfectly even traffic. A follow-up could introduce entropy (how
spread out is the distribution?) and the Gini coefficient (borrowed from
economics, where it measures income inequality — here it measures traffic
inequality) as complementary lenses on the same question.

**Factorio belt balancers.** Factorio players already have strong intuition for
"4-to-4 balanced" vs. "throughput unlimited" — two distinctions that map
directly onto strong connectivity and uniform stationary distribution. Belt
splitters have a parity-like constraint (inputs must equal outputs for a true
balancer), and the hidden bottleneck in a naïve layout is exactly the
one-diagonal trap from above. The stationary distribution framing applies
unchanged; only the physical metaphor shifts.
