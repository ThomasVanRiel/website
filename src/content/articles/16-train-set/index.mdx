### The track as a graph

<Scaffold>

A track is a collection of straight pieces, curves, and switches connecting them. Switches are the interesting part: they're the points where the train has a _choice_. Once we draw each segment as an edge and each junction as a node, the question becomes a question about a graph.

There's a subtlety the graph alone hides: every real piece has a "male" end and a "female" end, so a valid track has to alternate M → F → M → F at every joint. Two male ends or two female ends bumping into each other simply won't connect. That's a _parity_ constraint sitting on top of the graph — and it's exactly what adapter pieces are for.

### Connectivity

<Scaffold>
Introduce weak vs. strong connectivity with a concrete example before naming the terms.

- Weak connectivity: you can get between any two nodes if you ignore direction. The layout is one
  connected piece with no isolated sections.
- Strong connectivity: you can get between any two nodes going forward only — no reversing. The
  train can reach every segment without ever backing up.

A plain oval is strongly connected. Adding a one-way diagonal can break strong connectivity even
though the layout is still weakly connected.

This is the formal answer to research question 1. Name the terms after the intuitive explanation,
not before.

</Scaffold>

A single-direction train cannot back up out of a dead end, so reaching every segment imposes a real constraint on the layout. The relevant graph property here is strong connectivity, and — if we want every _segment_ covered rather than every junction — we drift into Eulerian-circuit territory.

### Eulerian circuits

<Scaffold>
An Eulerian circuit visits every edge exactly once and returns to the start. For a directed graph
this requires every node to have equal in-degree and out-degree.

## Simplifying the mathematics, running a virtual train

<Scaffold>
  Transition paragraph: graph theory tells us about structure, but not how much
  traffic each segment gets. For that we simulate. Frame the simulator as a
  digital twin: a virtual model that mirrors the physical toy, the same way
  engineers test a bridge in software before building it.
</Scaffold>

### Markov chains

<Scaffold>
When the train hits a switch, it picks a branch at random. The sequence of segments visited is
then a Markov chain: the next segment depends only on where the train is now, not how it got
there. That memorylessness is what makes the math tractable.

Each segment ends up with a long-run probability of being visited — the stationary distribution.
That probability is the traffic score for that segment.

</Scaffold>

Random switch settings turn the train's path into a random walk on the graph of segments. Each segment then has a long-run probability of being occupied — the stationary distribution of a Markov chain — and that probability is a natural "score" for the section. Busy junctions score high; isolated spurs score low.

The same idea shows up all over the place. The closest cousin to our train problem is Monopoly: which square gets landed on the most? It's a Markov chain on the board, and the answer (the orange properties) comes out of exactly the calculation we're about to do here. Google's original PageRank ranked the entire web by modelling a random surfer clicking from page to page. Predictive text on your phone guesses the next word the same way. Markov chains are how you ask "where does this end up, on average, if you let it run forever?"

### Monte Carlo, the digital twin

<Scaffold>
Monte Carlo: run the virtual train for thousands of steps, count visits, divide by total steps.
The history and analogies below are already written and should be kept.

Add one sentence explicitly naming this a digital twin: "Running thousands of virtual trains this
way is what engineers call a digital twin — a software model that mirrors a physical system
closely enough that you can ask questions of the model instead of the object."

</Scaffold>

The stationary distribution has a closed form, but a Monte Carlo simulation — just running a virtual train for a few thousand steps and counting visits — is far more intuitive, and gives us a useful sanity check against the analytical answer.

The technique is named after the casino in Monaco, and was invented in the 1940s at Los Alamos when physicists working on nuclear reactions realised some problems were easier to _simulate_ than to solve with pen and paper. The same trick is everywhere today: weather services run hundreds of slightly-different simulations to get probability forecasts, banks estimate the risk of a portfolio by rolling thousands of imaginary futures, animated films render realistic lighting by shooting millions of virtual rays of light, and AlphaGo picked its moves by playing out millions of random games in its head. The toy version anyone can try: estimate π by throwing virtual darts at a square and counting how many land inside an inscribed circle.

### How to read the figures

<Scaffold>
This subsection prepares the reader to interpret every figure in the Findings section. Write it
before the first figure appears — not after.

Key points to cover:

- Each segment has a bulge drawn alongside it, sized in proportion to its traffic score.
- The bulge is split into a left half and a right half — one per direction of travel.
- Symmetric bulge (equal halves): the train passes roughly equally in both directions.
- Lopsided bulge (one half much larger): traffic flows predominantly one way.
- Missing bulge on one side: that direction is never (or rarely) visited.

Stress that the figures are stochastic: each one is one run of the Monte Carlo simulation. The
reader should watch for a few seconds before drawing conclusions — the long-run pattern is what
matters, not the first few steps.

The existing prose below covers the left/right asymmetry well; keep it.

</Scaffold>

The natural way to surface these per-segment probabilities on the track itself is to draw a shape that grows alongside each segment in proportion to its traffic. Splitting that shape into a _left_ half and a _right_ half — one bulge per direction of travel — turns out to reveal more than the totals alone.

A _symmetric_ bulge means the segment is a two-way street: the train passes through it about as often in each direction. A _lopsided_ bulge means the segment is effectively one-directional — the layout funnels traffic through it in only one orientation. A plain oval shows up as 100% one-sided everywhere; a balloon track shows up as a symmetric trunk feeding a one-sided loop. So the visualisation isn't just an aesthetic display of the score — the symmetry pattern surfaces the directional structure of the layout for free.

<Scaffold>

### Quick tips for a good track

<Scaffold>
4-5 short, actionable rules for builders. Suggested starting point (from the old Key takeaways):

- More switches -> more even coverage. Every switch is a decision point that mixes the train.
- Spread switches around. Two adjacent switches give the train only one meaningful choice.
- Watch out for switches whose branches rejoin quickly — you're paying for a switch and getting
  a straight piece.
- Avoid dead ends if you want one-direction running.
- A loop with a spur beats a long straight line.
